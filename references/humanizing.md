# Removing AI writing patterns

Based on [Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing),
maintained by WikiProject AI Cleanup.

Apply this to anything a prospect will read: emails and LinkedIn messages. A draft that
reads as machine-written costs the reply, whatever the content says.

## Relationship to the Breakthrough voice rules

`context-assembly.md` carries Breakthrough's own banned phrases and words. That list is
narrower and product-specific. This one is general. **Both apply, and where they differ
the stricter wins.** Nothing here licenses a phrase the Breakthrough list bans.

## How to run it

Draft, then audit your own draft, then revise. Answer two questions honestly before you
hand anything over:

1. What in this makes it read as AI-generated?
2. Does the rewrite state any fact, name, number, date, or quote that was not in the
   retrieved source material?

The second is the one that matters most here. A fabricated detail about a real prospect
is worse than a bland sentence, because it goes out under the user's name.

Output only the final text. No draft, no audit notes, no summary of what you changed.

## Preserve the information, not the shape

Every claim survives the rewrite. Depth does not have to be uniform: compress the dull
parts, dwell where a person would, merge or split sentences freely. When keeping the
information and keeping the original structure pull apart, the information wins.

**Never invent facts.** No name, number, date, quote, or citation that is not in the
source. Swapping a vague claim for a specific one is allowed only when the specific comes
from the retrieved data. If a sentence needs a detail it does not have, write the plain
version or ask.

## Voice calibration

If the conversation contains the user's own prior writing, or they supply a sample, read
it first and match its habits: sentence length, vocabulary, paragraph openings,
punctuation, recurring phrases. Do not upgrade casual words or regularize deliberate
quirks.

A sample outranks the rules below, including the dash rule. If the user's own writing
uses em dashes, keep them at roughly that frequency. Matching the author beats scrubbing
the tell.

## Content patterns

**1. Undue emphasis on significance.** Watch: stands/serves as, is a testament, a
vital/crucial/pivotal role, underscores its importance, reflects broader, symbolizing,
setting the stage for, marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, indelible
mark, deeply rooted.

> The institute was established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of
> regional statistics.

becomes

> The institute was established in 1989, part of a wider decentralization of
> administrative functions.

**2. Undue emphasis on notability.** Watch: independent coverage, national media
outlets, written by a leading expert, active social media presence. Listing outlets
without context is padding. Keep the one you can say something about; drop the list.

**3. Superficial "-ing" analysis.** Watch: highlighting, underscoring, emphasizing,
ensuring, reflecting, contributing to, fostering, encompassing, showcasing. Participle
phrases tacked on the end to fake depth.

> The palette resonates with the region's natural beauty, symbolizing the local
> landscape, reflecting the community's connection to the land.

becomes

> The palette is blue, green, and gold, meant to evoke the local landscape.

**4. Promotional language.** Watch: boasts a, vibrant, rich (figurative), profound,
enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, nestled, in the heart of,
groundbreaking, renowned, breathtaking, stunning.

**5. Vague attribution.** Watch: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue,
some critics argue. If a real source exists, name it. Never invent one. An unsupported
claim gets cut, not decorated.

**6. "Challenges and future prospects" sections.** Watch: despite its... faces several
challenges, despite these challenges, future outlook. Formulaic. State the actual
problem and stop.

## Language and grammar

**7. AI vocabulary.** actually, additionally, align with, crucial, delve, emphasizing,
enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, key
(adjective), landscape (abstract), pivotal, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore,
valuable, vibrant. These cluster; one is nothing, four is a confession.

**8. Copula avoidance.** Watch: serves as, stands as, marks, represents, boasts,
features, offers. Use "is" and "has".

> Gallery 825 serves as the exhibition space and boasts over 3,000 square feet.

becomes

> Gallery 825 is the exhibition space. It has 3,000 square feet.

**9. Negative parallelism and tailing negation.** "Not only... but...", "It's not just
about X, it's Y". Also clipped fragments bolted on the end: "no guessing", "no wasted
motion". Write the real clause.

**10. Rule of three.** Forcing ideas into triplets to sound comprehensive. Two is
usually the honest number.

**11. Elegant variation.** Cycling synonyms for the same referent (the protagonist, the
main character, the central figure, the hero) because of repetition penalties. Repeat
the noun.

**12. False ranges.** "From X to Y" where X and Y are not on a scale.

**13. Passive voice and subjectless fragments.** "No configuration needed." "The results
are preserved automatically." Name the actor.

## Style

**14. No em dashes or en dashes.** This is a hard constraint, not a preference. The em
dash is the single most reliable tell. Replace each one, in rough order of preference: a
period, a comma, a colon, parentheses, or restructure. Catch spaced em dashes and double
hyphens used the same way.

Scan the final text for `—` and `–` before delivering. A hit means it is not done. The
only override is a user writing sample that uses them.

**15. Boldface.** Do not emphasize phrases mechanically.

**16. Inline-header lists.** Bulleted items that open with a bolded label and a colon.
Write the sentence instead.

**17. Title case in headings.** Use sentence case.

**18. Emojis.** None.

**19. Curly quotes.** Use straight quotes.

## Communication artifacts

**20. Chat residue.** "I hope this helps", "Of course!", "Certainly!", "You're
absolutely right!", "Would you like...", "Let me know if...". None of this belongs in a
message going to a prospect.

**21. Knowledge-cutoff and speculative gap-filling.** "As of my last update", "while
specific details are limited", "based on available information", "maintains a low
profile", "likely began". When the data does not say, say it does not say, or cut the
sentence. Do not dress a guess as fact. This one matters especially here: the gap is
usually a prospect whose record is thin, and the stock filler is exactly what gets
noticed.

**22. Sycophancy.** "Great question!", "You're absolutely right", "That's an excellent
point."

## Filler and hedging

**23. Filler phrases.** "in order to" becomes "to". "due to the fact that" becomes
"because". "at this point in time" becomes "now". "has the ability to" becomes "can".
"it is important to note that" becomes nothing.

**24. Excessive hedging.** "could potentially possibly be argued that it might have some
effect" becomes "may affect".

**25. Generic positive conclusions.** "The future looks bright." "Exciting times ahead."
Cut the paragraph. End on the last concrete fact.

**26. Hyphenated pair overuse.** third-party, cross-functional, data-driven,
decision-making, well-known, high-quality, real-time, long-term, end-to-end. Keep the
hyphen when the compound sits before the noun ("a high-quality report"); drop it when it
follows ("the report is high quality").

**27. Persuasive authority tropes.** "The real question is", "at its core", "in
reality", "what really matters", "fundamentally", "the deeper issue".

**28. Signposting.** "Let's dive in", "here's what you need to know", "let's break this
down". Do the thing instead of announcing it.

**29. Fragmented headers.** A heading followed by a one-line restatement of the heading.

**30. Diff-anchored writing.** Describing a thing by what it used to be instead of what
it is.

**31. Manufactured punchlines and staccato drama.** Every sentence landing like a
closer, or a run of short fragments stacked for effect. One short sentence for emphasis
is fine. Four in a row is engineered.

**32. Aphorism formulas.** "X is the Y of Z", "X becomes a trap", "the language of", "the
currency of", "the architecture of". Replace with the concrete claim it gestures at.

**33. Conversational rhetorical openers.** "Honestly?", "Look,", "Here's the thing",
"Let's be honest", used as a theatrical pause before an ordinary point. A person being
honest usually just says the thing.

## What not to flag

A good human writer hits several of these without any machine involvement. Do not gut
legitimate prose. On their own, these prove nothing:

Perfect grammar. Mixed casual and formal register. Plain or dry writing. Formal
vocabulary that is not on the §7 list. A letter-style opening or sign-off. One
transition word. Curly quotes alone (most editors auto-curl). One em dash alone. A
single short emphatic sentence. "Honestly" or "look" used mid-sentence. Unsourced
claims. Clean formatting.

Look for clusters, not isolated hits.

## Signs of human writing, which you should preserve

Specific, hard-to-fabricate detail. Mixed feelings and unresolved tension. Dated
references tied to a year or subculture. Editorial choices the writer could defend.
Variety in sentence length. Genuine asides and self-corrections.

In sales writing the first of these carries most of the weight. A real line from a real
call is the thing no generated message can imitate, and it is exactly what the rest of
this list is protecting.
